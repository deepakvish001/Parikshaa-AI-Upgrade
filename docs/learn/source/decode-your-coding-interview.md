## **The Typical Tech Interview**

Let's talk about what usually happens in a regular tech interview. It's not as scary as it sounds!

First, a recruiter will call you. This is like a warm-up chat. They want to know about your skills, what you do now, and what you want in your next job. If they like what they hear, they'll find a team that's interested in you. If both sides are excited, you move forward.

Next, there's a technical phone interview. It depends on the company, but it often starts with chatting about your experience. Then they might ask a simple question like "What's a thread?" Finally, they'll throw in some coding problems.

If you do well on the phone interview, you might get another phone interview or an invite to visit the company's office. But if it doesn't go so well, don't worry – you can try again later.

Onsite interviews are like a series of mini-tests. There are about 5 rounds, and each one checks different things. It could be coding skills, knowing how to design systems, or even how you behave in a team.

If you impress during the onsite interviews, they might offer you a job. But here's the thing: even if you do really well, they might still choose someone else. That's just how it goes sometimes.

So, remember, a tech interview is like getting ready for a cool performance. Practice, learn, and show them what you've got!

- In an onsite interview, you'll write code on a whiteboard. But some might let you use a computer. Just make sure you know the rules before you start. If it's whiteboard time, practice writing on whiteboards.

### **Helpful Tips for Any Interview**

Every interview is unique, but here are some tips that always work:

1. **Ask clarifying questions to understand what is being asked:**
Before you start solving a problem, make sure you understand exactly what the interviewer wants. If the problem isn't clear, ask questions to get a better picture.
2. **Do not write code until you understand the problem and have thought of a solution:**
It's important not to rush into coding right away. Take your time to fully understand the problem and come up with a plan or solution in your mind before you start typing code.
3. **Keep talking during the interview. The interviewer wants to hear as much of your thought process as possible:**
Don't keep your thoughts to yourself. Speak out loud about what you're thinking and how you're approaching the problem. The interviewer is interested in how you tackle challenges.
4. **If you need to pause talking so you can think, that's perfectly fine, just explain that to them:**
It's okay to take a moment to think. If you need to pause, let the interviewer know that you're taking a moment to gather your thoughts. Communication is key.
5. **If you are confused or are unsure about the next steps, ask questions:**
If you're not sure what to do next, don't be afraid to ask for clarification. It's better to get clear instructions than to proceed in the wrong direction.
6. **For example, I will commonly ask if my approach to the solution sounds good and whether it's fine to proceed to code:**
It's a good practice to check with the interviewer if your planned approach is on the right track before you start writing code. This shows that you're thoughtful and considerate.
7. **Note that there is some danger to asking questions. You don't want to give the impression that you are helpless and can't lead the conversation. You need to strike the right balance:**
While asking questions is important, you also want to show that you're capable of leading the discussion. It's about finding a balance between seeking guidance and demonstrating your problem-solving skills.
8. **Periodically check-in with the interviewer as sometimes you might be talking so much that they don't have the room to interject:**
While it's great to explain your thought process, make sure you also give the interviewer a chance to provide input or ask questions. Checking in with them shows you're aware of the conversation flow.
9. **Use the language you are most comfortable with to code:**
Write your code in the programming language you're most confident in. It's not about using fancy languages; it's about showcasing your coding skills effectively.
10. **You can't use every language as the interviewer may not be familiar with it:**
While you might know many programming languages, the interviewer might not be familiar with all of them. Stick to languages that are commonly used and understood.
11. **Some languages are a pain to use in an interview, and if possible, I'd avoid them. For example, Objective-C is very verbose and lacks support for many tasks performed in an interview (ex. it's very tedious to work with strings in Objective-C):**
Some languages might make solving problems more challenging due to their syntax or limitations. It's wise to choose a language that allows you to express your solutions clearly and efficiently.

These tips are like your secret weapons to perform well in any interview. They show your problem-solving skills, your communication abilities, and your adaptability – all of which are essential for success.

## **Breaking Down Learning: Example for "Tree" Data Structure**

Okay, let's break down how you'd study and create flashcards for the "Tree" data structure:

**Question1: What is a tree? How would you code it?**
Answer: Imagine a tree like a family tree – there's a main person (root), and they have kids (children). Each child can also have their own kids. To code a tree, you'd use nodes to represent people, and each node points to its children.

**Question2: What is the difference between a tree and a graph?**
Answer: A tree is a special kind of graph. A tree can't have loops or cycles – it's like a family tree with no repeated members. But a graph can have cycles.

**Question3: What is a root? Talk about its properties.**
Answer: The root is the very first node of the tree. It's the top boss! A root doesn't have a parent because, well, it's at the top.

**Question4: What is a child? Talk about its properties.**
Answer: A child is a node that comes after another node – its parent. It's like a kid in a family. Every child has a parent, but not all nodes are parents.

**Question5: What is a Binary Search Tree? What are its properties?**
Answer: Think of a Binary Search Tree like a well-organized library. All nodes on the left have smaller values than their parent, and nodes on the right have bigger values. This makes searching quick.

## After spending some time thinking about the problem, we can make two quick observations:

- We need to find two indices in the `numbers` array. That means **we will have to loop through the array to search for numbers**.
- The numbers could be anywhere in the array (maybe the start, maybe the end), so in the worst case, we will have to go through the whole array. That makes this algorithm at least O(N).

## With the observations in mind, let's take the example `[-3,0,1,5,1,3]` and start thinking about how we can go through the array:

- **We need to start somewhere, so let's start at `index 0`, which gives us the number `3`.**
- We have one number, but we need two. Lets keep going through the array trying each one:
    - If we try `numbers[1] == -3`, `3 + 0 = -3; -3 != 4`
    - If we try `numbers[2] == 1`, `3 + 1 = -2; -2 != 4`
    - If we try `numbers[3] == 5`, `3 + 5 = 2; 2 != 4`
    - If we keep going, we will see that we can't find another number that, combined with `3`, adds up to the target `4`.
- We have exhausted all possibilities from `index 0`, which means we have to move to `index 1` and repeat this process.
- Eventually, we find that `numbers[2] + numbers[5] == 4`.

## From this simple exercise, we have already derived an algorithm:

- Start at index 0 for the first number
- Then, try all other indices to see whether the first number adds up to the second number.
    - If they do...return the two indices.
    - If they do not...increment the index of the first number and repeat...

## Here is the algorithm written down:

```
def twoSum(numbers, target):
    for index1 in range(len(numbers)):
        for index2 in range(index1 + 1, len(numbers)):
            if numbers[index1] + numbers[index2] == target:
                return [index1, index2]
```

> Note:This process may sound simplistic to you, but there is a powerful lesson here. You can make a simple observation like "I need two numbers from an array," and begin by doing the most straightforward thing possible: begin at index 0. Then, let the algorithm evolve from there.I can credit this method to helping me pass the Google hiring committee. ## **Let's use the `O(N*log(N))` observation**. We have an array of numbers, so sorting seems like a good option to try.

When we sort the array we get: `[-3,0,1,1,3,5]`.

Cool. We sorted the array, but what does that give us?

## We formed a new property for the input: the numbers are ordered from small-to-large. **Can we somehow exploit this property?**

- Let's begin by repeating what we did above: **start at index 0.**
    - `numbers[0] + numbers[1] == -3 + 0 = -3; -3 != 4`
    - `numbers[0] + numbers[2] == -3 + 1 = -2; -2 != 4`
    - `numbers[0] + numbers[3] == -3 + 1 = -2; -2 != 4`
    - `numbers[0] + numbers[4] == -3 + 3 = 0; 0 != 4`
    - `numbers[0] + numbers[5] == -3 + 5 = 2; 2 != 4`
- While we didn't find an answer, we did find a pattern:
    - `3 + 0 = -3`
    - `3 + 1 = -2`
    - `3 + 1 = -2`
    - `3 + 3 = 0`
    - `3 + 5 = 2`
- Let's clarify some of the things we are noticing:
    - Every time we increment the second index the sum will increase.
    - If we start at index 0, the highest sum we can achieve is `2`. That means that we only have to look at at the first number (`index[0]`) and the last number (`index[5]`) to figure out how close we are to the `target` (`4`). If `index[0] + index[5] < target` that means that there is NO way that `index[0]` will ever add up to the `target`: the sum will always be too small. On the other hand, if `index[0] + index[5] > target` there is NO way that `index[5]` can be used to add up to the `target`: the sum will always be too large. In other words, we only need to compare two numbers to go through the array.

## Let's recap:

1. **By starting at `index 0` and listing all the possibilities, we found a pattern.**
2. Then, by analyzing the pattern, we realized that we only need to compare the first and the last number to find the target.

> Note: Leaping to this conclusion is not always straightforward. However, with a lot of practice, and by using the "start at index 0" tactic, you will be able to find these types of insights.
> 

## These observations give us an `O(N*log(N))` algorithm:

```
def twoSum(numbers, target):
    def comparisonFunction(number1, number2):
        if number1 <= number2:
            return -1
        elif number1 > number2:
            return 1
        else:
            return 0
    
    numbers.sort(key=comparisonFunction)
    firstIndex = 0
    lastIndex = len(numbers) - 1

    while firstIndex < lastIndex:
        _sum = numbers[firstIndex] + numbers[lastIndex]
        if _sum == target:
            return [firstIndex, lastIndex]
        elif _sum > target:
            lastIndex -= 1
        else:
            firstIndex += 1

# Example usage
numbers = [2, 7, 11, 15]
target = 9
result = twoSum(numbers, target)
print(result)  # This will output [0, 1]
```

### **However, this is the wrong answer!**

The question requires us to return the *indices* of the numbers. We sorted the numbers, which changed the original indices!

If the question asked us to return two numbers that add up to `target`, or asked us to return whether there *exist* two numbers that add up to the `target`, this approach would be perfectly fine. That being said, it is still possible to solve the question using this method; you will just have to map the original indices to the sorted indices.

**A mistake such as this is a great lesson!** You can now put this observation in your bag of tools: "If the question requires the original order, then sorting, which changes the order, might not be the optimal solution."

- `numberOne = numbers[0] == -3`

## **How can you utilize these 4 variables?**

- Checking whether `numberOne` is in the `numberSet` doesn't give us anything.
- Checking whether `target` exists in the set also doesn't give us much because we are looking for TWO numbers that add up to the target — not one.
- Let's formalize "two numbers that add up to the target" by writing an equation: `numberOne + numberTwo = target`
- We have `numberOne` and we have `target` so: `numberTwo = target - numberOne`
- In other words, given `numberOne` and `target`, we can figure out what number we need to find in the array. The `numberSet` gives us the ability to find a number in `O(1)` time.

We have the basis for a solution: go through the array and keep checking whether `numberOne - target` is within the set. If it is, we found a pair of numbers that add up to the target.

## Nevertheless, there are still two issues:

1. We need to return the indices of the two numbers, and currently, we are storing only numbers in the set.
2. We need to avoid an edge-case with duplicate numbers. For example, if the target is `4` and we have the number `2` in the array, we don't want to reuse the same number `2` for the solution.

Fixing #1 is straighforward. We can use a hashtable to store a mapping of each number to the index.

Fixing #2 is less obvious, but we can avoid checking for the same number in the hashtable by checking the hashtable *before* updating it with the new number.

## This gives us the following pseudocode:

```
def twoSum(numbers, target):
    hashtable = {}  # Create an empty dictionary

    for i in range(len(numbers)):
        numberOne = numbers[i]
        if target - numberOne in hashtable:
            return [hashtable[target - numberOne], i]
        else:
            hashtable[numberOne] = i

# Example usage
numbers = [2, 7, 11, 15]
target = 9
result = twoSum(numbers, target)
print(result)  # This will output [0, 1]
```

**...Then highlight the key parts** (ask yourself questions about each key part and analyze it):

    - As a result of each integer in the array being bound by `[1,array.length]`, and because there can be duplicates, there’s a chance that some integers do not appear in the original array. We need to find all the elements that do not appear!

An **array of integers**:

Example 2:

**Given `[1,2,2]`, how do we find the missing number 3?**

First, where does the number “3” even come from?

- All the numbers in the array are bound by `[1,array.length]`. If there were no missing numbers the array would be `[1,2,3]`. That’s where the “3” comes from.

In other words, we know all the numbers we expect to see (`[1,2,3]`) in the input array, and we just need to find the ones that are not included in it.

**We could store all the expected numbers (`[1,2,3]`) into a set. Then, as we iterate through the input, we can remove all the numbers we saw from the set. Any leftovers are the missing numbers!**

Here’s the code:

```jsx
def findMissingNumbers(numbers):
    # Create a set populated with numbers from 1 to len(numbers)
    allNumbers = set(range(1, len(numbers) + 1))

    # Remove any number we see from the set
    for number in numbers:
        allNumbers.discard(number)

    # Convert the set to a sorted list (missing numbers)
    return sorted(allNumbers)

# Example usage
numbers = [4, 2, 7, 1, 6]
missing_numbers = findMissingNumbers(numbers)
print(missing_numbers)  # This will output [3, 5]
```

If you ever see a phrase like "find duplicates" in an algorithm description, then a set, which can track unique values, is always a solid bet!

**The time complexity of this solution is `O(N)`, and the space complexity is also `O(N)`.**

The algorithm could go through the following steps:

1. **Iterate through the numbers array and perform swaps.**
    - If we see that the number at `numbers[i]` is not equal to `i+1`, then swap it to its correct position: `numbers[numbers[i]-1] = numbers[i]`.
        - However, only make a swap if `numbers[i]` is not already in its correct position. There could be duplicates!
    - Else, if we see that the number at `numbers[i]` is equal to `i+1`, then keep going.
2. **Then, go through the numbers array again, and if we see that a number is not in the correct index, then it’s a missing number!**

## **Writing The Solution**

All the hard work is done! **Make sure to avoid errors by constantly checking** that each line you write makes sense and wouldn’t cause bugs.

```jsx
def findMissingNumbers(numbers):
    i = 0
    while i < len(numbers):
        currentNumber = numbers[i]

        if currentNumber == i + 1:
            i += 1
        else:
            if numbers[currentNumber - 1] == currentNumber:
                i += 1
            else:
                previousNumber = numbers[currentNumber - 1]
                numbers[currentNumber - 1] = currentNumber
                numbers[i] = previousNumber

    missingNumbers = []
    for i in range(len(numbers)):
        currentNumber = numbers[i]
        if currentNumber != i + 1:
            missingNumbers.append(i + 1)

    return missingNumbers

# Example usage
numbers = [2, 3, 1, 8, 2, 3]
missing_numbers = findMissingNumbers(numbers)
print(missing_numbers)  # This will output [4, 5, 6, 7]
```

def numberOfUniquePaths(matrix):
    row = 0
    column = 0
    numberOfUniquePathsHelper(matrix, row, column)

def numberOfUniquePathsHelper(matrix, row, column):
    # Handle "we could pass the boundary of the matrix"
    number_of_rows = len(matrix)
    if row >= number_of_rows:
        return 0
    number_of_columns = len(matrix[row])
    if column >= number_of_columns:
        return 0

    if matrix[row][column] == 1:

**The code works, but it is not efficient.**

Every single time we call `numberOfUniquePathsHelper` there are two recursive function calls: we either go “down” by recursing with `row+1`, or we “move to the right” by recursing with `column+1`. For a N - M (`numberOfRows - numberOfColumns`) matrix, this results a **factorial time complexity**: `O((N+M)! / (N! - M!))`.

How can we improve this?

---

**We need to find inefficiencies and figure out how to improve them.**

If we scan the code, we will find **two inefficient lines**:

```jsx
numberOfUniquePathsHelper(matrix, row+1, column)
numberOfUniquePathsHelper(matrix, row, column+1)
```

**These lines form a brute-force algorithm that tries every position in the matrix.** At an initial glance, there doesn’t seem to be much room for improvement: we need to try every position because we don’t know which one will have an obstacle and which one will not. However, there is a lurking problem underneath that we can solve!

**As we try every row/column pair in the matrix, we are sometimes re-visiting the same position multiple times. This is called “overlapping subproblems.”** In fact, it’s such a typical pattern in brute-force solutions that you will instantly notice it with experience.

For example, look at these two paths:

![8.png](Introduction%20to%20Decode%20Your%20Coding%20Interview/8.png)

They both visit the (1,1) position (i.e. the blue square), which results in them repeating the same steps: **(1,1) -> (2,1) -> (2,2) -> (2,3)**.

Visiting the path **(1,1) -> (2,1) -> (2,2) -> (2,3)** is a subproblem and we are overlapping it in these two examples.

---

**We can fix the overlapping subproblems issue by storing previously computed results.** Then, if we reencounter the same subproblem, we will return the previously computed result.

Here is the pseudocode for this very common pattern:

```jsx
previous_results = {}

def solve_problem(input):
    if input in previous_results:
        return previous_results[input]
    else:
        output = do_some_math(input)
        previous_results[input] = output
        return output
```

For our problem, the “input” is `row` and `column`, and the “output” is `uniquePaths`. **We need to create another matrix that stores the `uniquePaths` for each `row`/`column` pair, and then use this matrix to store and retrieve results.** We should now be able to write code for the final solution!

## **Writing The Solution**

All the hard work is done! **Make sure to avoid errors by constantly checking** that each line you write makes sense and wouldn’t cause bugs.

```jsx
def numberOfUniquePaths(matrix):
    if not matrix or len(matrix) == 0 or len(matrix[0]) == 0:
        return 1

    # Initialize a `numberOfRows * numberOfColumns` matrix of -1 values
    previous_results = [[-1] * len(matrix[0]) for _ in range(len(matrix))]
    return numberOfUniquePathsHelper(matrix, 0, 0, previous_results)

def numberOfUniquePathsHelper(matrix, row, column, previous_results):
    number_of_rows = len(matrix)
    if row >= number_of_rows:
        return 0
    number_of_columns = len(matrix[row])
    if column >= number_of_columns:
        return 0

    if matrix[row][column] == 1:
        return 0

    # If we wrote a value in `previous_results` already, return it.
    if previous_results[row][column] != -1:
        return previous_results[row][column]

    if row == number_of_rows - 1 and column == number_of_columns - 1:
        return 1

    unique_paths = 0

    unique_paths += numberOfUniquePathsHelper(matrix, row + 1, column, previous_results)
    unique_paths += numberOfUniquePathsHelper(matrix, row, column + 1, previous_results)

    # Store the computed results so we can reuse the value!
    previous_results[row][column] = unique_paths

    return unique_paths
```

- We can iterate `list.length - k` times to find the start of our output list.
- Throughout this process, we need to keep track of the original head ("head1"), the original tail ("tail2"), the next head ("head2"), and the node before the next head (“tail1”).
- Once we have all the nodes, re-arrange them!

**We also encountered a lot of sub-problems:**

2. Iterate a linked list a specific number of nodes.

```jsx
class ListNode:
    def __init__(self, value=0, next=None):
        self.value = value
        self.next = next

def rotate_list(head1, k):

    def list_length(head):
        length = 0
        while head:
            length += 1
            head = head.next
        return length

    def find_nodes(head, x):
        prev = None
        current = head
        for _ in range(x):
            prev = current
            current = current.next
        return prev, current

    # Handle edge-case
    if not head1:
        return None

    # Subproblem 1: get the size of a singly linked list
    list_length_val = list_length(head1)

    # Subproblem 3: handle `k >= list_length`
    k = k % list_length_val

    # Handle edge-case
    if k == 0:
        return head1

    # Subproblem 2: iterate a linked list a specific number of nodes
    number_of_nodes_to_skip = list_length_val - k
    tail1, head2 = find_nodes(head1, number_of_nodes_to_skip)

    # Find `tail2`
    _, tail2 = find_nodes(head1, list_length_val - 1)

    # Subproblem 4: handle list pointer changes.
    tail1.next = None
    tail2.next = head1

    return head2
```

    def __init__(self, value=0, next=None):

        self.next = next

    while head:
        length += 1
        head = head.next
    return length

    previous_node = None
    node = head
    for _ in range(number_of_nodes_to_skip):
        previous_node = node
        node = node.next
    return previous_node, node

def rotate_list(head1, k):

        return None

    list_length = length_of_list(head1)

    k = k % list_length

    if k == 0:
        return head1

    number_of_nodes_to_skip = list_length - k

    tail2 = find_nodes(head1, list_length - 1)[1]

    tail1.next = None
    tail2.next = head1

    return head2

> Note: If you practice a lot, and build a knowledge bank of various algorithm scenarios, you will be able to quickly make progress when looking at a new algorithm because you will naturally know the answers to the algorithm's subproblems.

Each character has a unique identifier or a “hash.” As a result, **we can utilize a `Set` or a `Hashtable` to store each character**, and if we see that the character was encountered before, we return `false` (the string does not have unique characters):

```jsx
def has_no_repeating_characters(string):
    char_set = set()

    for char in string:
        # If char_set contains the character already,
        # then we have duplicate characters!
        if char in char_set:
            return False

        # Add each character to the char_set
        char_set.add(char)

    return True  # Found no duplicate characters
```

> 

We solved one subproblem of the larger problem. Now, we can take this subproblem and apply it to the larger problem.

However, there is **another subproblem: given a string, determine all substrings of the string**. This forms a common, brute-force `O(N^2)` solution. (For simplicity, we are ignoring time complexity of `string.substring` and assuming it's O(1).)

```jsx
def find_substrings(string, substring_reporter):
    # Iterate through the whole string
    for start_index in range(len(string)):
        
        # Iterate through each substring - starting from `startIndex`
        for end_index in range(start_index, len(string)):
            
            # Form the substring
            substring = string[start_index:end_index + 1]
            
            # Report back the found substring
            substring_reporter(substring)
```

We can now combine these two subproblems to solve the original problem:

```jsx
def length_of_longest_substring(string):
    max_length = 0

    # Subproblem 2: find all substrings
    def substring_reporter(substring):
        nonlocal max_length

        # Subproblem 1: check whether the substring has unique characters
        if has_no_repeating_characters(substring):

            # Since the `substring` has no repeating characters,
            # consider it as the longest substring without repeating characters
            max_length = max(max_length, len(substring))

    find_substrings(string, substring_reporter)

    return max_length
```

This is an **`O(N^3)` time complexity** solution:

- O(N) for the first loop.
- O(N) for the second loop.
- O(N) to search whether all characters in the substring are unique.

Looking at this problem from the perspective of multiple subproblems might look unfamiliar. However, we were trying to show that **many problems are composed of sub-problems**. As you solve more algorithms, your brain will automatically recognize these patterns and it will allow you to quickly find a solution.

We can re-write the brute-force solution in a more familiar format:

```jsx
def length_of_longest_substring(string):
    max_length = 0

    # Iterate through the whole string
    for start_index in range(len(string)):
        
        # Iterate through each substring - starting from `start_index`
        for end_index in range(start_index, len(string)):
            
            # Check if the substring has repeating characters
            has_repeating_characters = False
            char_set = set()
            for i in range(start_index, end_index + 1):
                character = string[i]
                if character in char_set:
                    has_repeating_characters = True
                    break
                char_set.add(character)
                
            if not has_repeating_characters:
                max_length = max(max_length, end_index - start_index + 1)

    return max_length
```

---

Can we do better?

Let’s consider other possibilities:

- `O(N^2)`
    - A decent candidate. However, that is still very inefficient and usually interviewers seek more optimal solutions.
- `O(N*log(N))`
    - Usually O(N*log(N)) implies sorting or using a data structure such as a heap. There are string questions that use heaps, but this wouldn’t be my first choice.
- `O(N)`
    - As we determined in “Understand The Problem” section, this is the quickest we could solve the algorithm because we have to consider the full length of the string. **Let’s consider this time complexity!**

**If we had to loop through the string once, what information can we collect while we iterate?**

We need to determine if a substring has unique characters, so **we could use a set and add characters to it**. If we encounter a duplicate character, the current substring is no longer valid and we need to start over:

```jsx
def length_of_longest_substring(string):
    char_set = set()
    max_length = 0

    # Track the start of the substring
    start_index = 0

    for end_index in range(len(string)):
        character = string[end_index]

        # Found a duplicate character
        if character in char_set:

            # Reset the algorithm. Begin search for a new string.
            char_set = set()
            start_index = end_index

        # Track all of the encountered characters
        char_set.add(character)

        max_length = max(max_length, end_index - start_index + 1)

    return max_length
```

**This code works for some cases, but it fails for others!** In fact, it fails for our example, **why?**

- Try running through this code with the example string “abcad” and you will get a `maxLength` of 3 (“abc”), while the `maxLength` is supposed to be 4 (“bcad").
- When the duplicate “a” is encountered, we move the startIndex to “index 3.” However, **we should be moving `startIndex` to "index 1”** because that’s is the next valid substring (no duplicate “a”).

> Note: “we should be moving startIndex to 'index 1’” is a crucial insight that only presented itself after using an example and working through the problem.
> 

What is happening?

- The code above ignores an important property of the problem: **when we encounter a duplicate character, we need to move `startIndex` to the the index AFTER the last time we encountered the duplicate character**. In the code above, we move the `startIndex` to the current index (`endIndex`).

In other words, **our solution above is perfectly fine EXCEPT that `startIndex` needs to be set differently**.

Can we track the “last time we encountered a duplicate character”?

- Yes. We can use a dictionary and map each character to its index.

**With a dictionary tracking the last index, we can fix the bug with the `startIndex` and rewrite our code for the final solution!**

> Note: We just “fumbled” our way to a solution. Step-by-step we made mistakes, and then figured out how to fix them.Whenever you have an idea on how to solve a problem, try to play around with it by going through the example. If it works, you’ve solved the algorithm! If it doesn’t work, you will have learned a new important property of the algorithm problem. Then, you can use your knowledge of data structures and algorithms to figure out how to account for this new algorithm property.
> 

## **Writing The Solution**

All the hard work is done! Make sure to avoid errors by constantly making sure that each line you write makes sense and wouldn’t cause bugs (ex. forgetting to assign an index).

Final code for the solution:

```jsx
def length_of_longest_substring(string):
    character_to_last_index = {}
    max_length = 0
    start_index = 0

    for end_index in range(len(string)):
        character = string[end_index]

        # Found a duplicate character
        if character in character_to_last_index:

            # Ignore the duplicate character by moving the `start_index` of substring
            start_index = max(start_index, character_to_last_index[character] + 1)

        # Track the last index of all encountered characters
        character_to_last_index[character] = end_index

        max_length = max(max_length, end_index - start_index + 1)

    return max_length
```

> Note:The Math.max in line 13 is also a part that’s easy to get wrong. If you don’t include it, then there is a chance that startIndex gets set to an index that is lower than the current startIndex! That’s a bug.There are a couple of ways you’d remember to write this: either you’d naturally notice the chance for the bug, or you’d have to step-by-step walk through the code by using examples and notice the bug that way. The former requires natural intuition gained through a lot of practice. The latter requires constant vigilance to try to find errors in your code!
> 

## **Testing / Reflection**

Questions:

- What is the time complexity?
    - `O(N)`.
- What is the space complexity?
    - `O(1)`. This is easy to get wrong. `O(1)` means constant space, and here we will utilize constant space because our character set is limited to Extended ASCII. As a result, the amount of data we store during the execution of the algorithm is bound by the number of Extended ASCII characters (256).
- Can we improve our solution?
    - Kind-of. We could use a more efficient data structure, like an array of 256 integers. The time/space complexity wouldn’t change, but its performance should be better.
- What are some interesting observations from this problem?
    - We relied on our knowledge of "computer science” (*what is a character?*) and data structures to quickly identify that we could store characters in a set or a hashtable to quickly identify whether they are unique.
        - **This shows how important it is to study terminology.** A lot of data structures can be helpful in very specific scenarios, and if you know those scenarios, you will be able to quickly identify them when you have a new algorithm problem to solve during an interview. Any time saved can be the difference between nailing the interview and fumbling it.
    - We used “two pointers” (`startIndex` and `endIndex`), or some would reference it as a “sliding window,” to solve the problem. There are lots of other string problems that track a `startIndex` and an `endIndex`.
- We went through a lot of code in "Understand The Problem" section. You probably wouldn't do this in an interview. ## **Coming Up With The Solution**

The first step is to **think of examples**.

The example from the problem description is great, however, **there are a few other edge-cases to consider**:

- A root that is NULL.
- A root that is also the leaf node (no children).

With the edge-cases considered, let’s use the example from the problem description to think of a solution:

![2.png](Introduction%20to%20Decode%20Your%20Coding%20Interview/2%201.png)

One of the first things I like to point out for a tree question is that **a tree has recursive properties**. For example, if we choose to recurse `left`, the function's root becomes the `left` node, and we are looking at a brand new subtree:

![3.png](Introduction%20to%20Decode%20Your%20Coding%20Interview/3%201.png)

...this subtree now looks like a brand new problem that must be solved in the same was as if you started from **`Node 1`**. In other words, **it’s recursive**!

Similar to how we used the tactic of “start at index 0” for the previous algorithm problems, for trees we can use a tactic of “start at root.” **If we start at the root, we can temporarily ignore the rest of the tree**. This let’s us focus on looking at three data points: the root, left node, and right node:

![4.png](Introduction%20to%20Decode%20Your%20Coding%20Interview/4%201.png)

Given this tree problem of three nodes, how can we populate the `next` nodes?

- From `root` we have access to `left`, `right`, and `next`.
- We can take `root.left.next` and assign it `root.right`.
- We don’t need to make any other assignments because the other `next` nodes already point to `NULL` by default.

![5.png](Introduction%20to%20Decode%20Your%20Coding%20Interview/5%201.png)

This can be expressed via the following pseudocode:

```
def point_to_next(root):
    root.left.next = root.right
```

At this point, we have achieved everything we need from **`Node 1`**, so let’s move to the next layer by moving to **`Node 2`**:

![6.png](Introduction%20to%20Decode%20Your%20Coding%20Interview/6%201.png)

Given this tree problem, and a starting point from **`Node 2`**, how can we populate the `next` nodes?

- From **`Node 2`** we have access to `left`, `right`, and `next`.
- Again, we can take `root.left.next` and assign it `root.right`.

Performing that operation gives us the following diagram:

![7.png](Introduction%20to%20Decode%20Your%20Coding%20Interview/7%201.png)

So far, this can be express via pseudocode like this:

```
def point_to_next(root):
    root.left.next = root.right
    point_to_next(root.left)  # recurse by going left
```

This looks great, but we see that **`Node 3`** children need an assignment too!

- We need a way to get to **`Node 3`**.
    - If we recurse to **`Node 4`**, we won’t have access to **`Node 3`**, so that won’t work.
    - If we look at the diagram, there are two ways to get to **`Node 3`**:
        1. We can get there from **`Node 1`** by using the `right` node.
        2. We can get there from **`Node 2`** by using the `next` node.
    - Since we are already at **`Node 2`**, let’s try using the `next` node.
- **We should also notice that there is a pattern**: we modify a node, then we traverse to the `next` node, and repeat. This forms a loop!

If we combine all the knowledge so far, we can form the following pseudocode:

```
def point_to_next(root):
    while root:
        root.left.next = root.right
        root = root.next  # go to the next node in the same level
    point_to_next(root.left)
```

However, this code has a bug! When the loop is done, `root.left` will point to `NULL`, and that will prevent us from getting to **`Node 4`**. To fix, **we need to introduce a new variable** that will save the original `root`:

```
def point_to_next(root):
    current_node = root
    while current_node:
        current_node.left.next = current_node.right
        current_node = current_node.next
    point_to_next(root.left)
```

Running the code will get us the following diagram:

![8.png](Introduction%20to%20Decode%20Your%20Coding%20Interview/8%201.png)

As we can see, **`Node 5`** does not point to **`Node 6`**, which means we have another bug!

> Note: You are coming up with a solution, not writing the final code. It’s perfectly fine to stumble and miss an edge-case. This is why you are going step-by-step: so you can make sure you fully understand the problem!
> 

Let's re-trace back to when we were at

**`Node 2:`**

![9.png](Introduction%20to%20Decode%20Your%20Coding%20Interview/9.png)

Given this state of the problem, how can we assign **`Node 6`** to **`Node 5`**?

- We have access to **`Node 5`** via `root.right`.
- We have access to **`Node 6`** via `root.next.left`.
- So we need to make a new assignment: `root.right.next = root.next.left`.

![10.png](Introduction%20to%20Decode%20Your%20Coding%20Interview/10.png)

This completes the chain and we can form our final pseudocode:

```
def point_to_next(root):
    current_node = root
    
    while current_node:
        # Connect the children to each other
        current_node.left.next = current_node.right

        if current_node.next:
            # Connect the right child to the right sub-tree
            current_node.right.next = current_node.next.left

        # Keep the loop going
        current_node = current_node.next

    # Recurse to the next level to repeat
    point_to_next(root.left)
```

> Note: Notice how we slowly broke the problem down into multiple stages. We didn’t try to handle all the cases all at once. We started at the root, and we figured out how to handle that. Then we moved to the next node, and we figured out how to handle that. Going through these steps is perfectly fine during an interview! It’s a natural part of problem-solving. If you try to handle all the cases all at once, your mind will explode, you will get frustrated, and then you will freak out. That, or you will make a mistake that you won’t realize until you finish writing the final code that was supposed to be correct. The only issue with these steps is time: during an interview, you will have to move quickly. Nevertheless, don’t worry about the time until you are comfortable with your algorithm skills.
> 

## **Writing The Solution**

All the hard work is done! **Make sure to avoid errors by continually checking** that each line you write makes sense and wouldn’t cause bugs (ex. accessing a `NULL` node).

```
def point_to_next(root):
    if root is None:
        return None
    if root.left is None:
        return root
    
    current_node = root
    while current_node is not None:
        current_node.left.next = current_node.right
        if current_node.next is not None:
            current_node.right.next = current_node.next.left
        current_node = current_node.next
    
    point_to_next(root.left)
    
    return root
```

# Jump Game

**Given an array of non-negative integers where each element represents the max number of indices you can jump from that element, write a function that determines whether you can reach the last index. You are initially positioned at the first index of the array.**

Example 1:

```
Input: [2,3,1,0,1]
Output: true
Explanation: Jump from index 0, to index 1. Then, jump 3 steps from index 1, to the last index.
```

Example 2:

```
Input: [1,3,0,1,0,5]
Output: false
Explanation: The max index you can reach is index 4, but you need to reach index 5. You can reach index 4 by jumping from index 0, to index 1, and then jumping 3 steps to index 4.
```

## **Understand The Problem**

Same process as we went through with Two Sum…

Let’s read the problem statement so we can get an overall understanding:

- **Given an array of non-negative integers where each element represents the max number of indices you can jump from that element, write a function that determines whether you can reach the last index.** You are initially positioned at the first index of the array.

...Then highlight the key parts:

- Given an **array** of **non-negative integers** where each element represents **the max number of indices** you can jump **from that element**, write a function that **determines whether you can reach the last index**. You are **initially positioned at the first index** of the array.
- The input is an array of random, *non-negative* integers.
- The description doesn’t explicitly say to use a boolean for the output, but we can see from the examples that we must use a boolean.
- The numbers in the array represent the *max* number of indices. If the number is 4, we can also jump 0 or 1 or 2 or 3 or 4 indices.
- We start at index 0.

From this information, we can form a basic idea of how the function will look like:

```
def jump_game(numbers):
    return True
```

Let’s analyze the input.

**Array of non-negative integers**:

- Could the array be empty?
    - Maybe. It's an edge-case. Let's return `true` if the array is empty. However, this is debatable! It’s a great thing to bring up in an interview.
- Could the array have one integer?
    - Yes. In that case, we are already at the last index, so we can return `true`.
- Is it sorted?
    - No, but it might be for some inputs.
- Could the array have negative numbers?
    - No. The array contains only non-negative integers like 0, 1, 2...

Let’s analyze the output.

**Boolean**:

- Not much to analyze here, but it is worth re-stating what we are trying to accomplish: if we can reach the last index (`numbers.length - 1`), we return `true`, else return `false`.

> Note: Notice how simple these questions are. Yet they are so crucial. Any change in the answers can mean the difference between solving the algorithm and getting it wrong.
> 

Other questions to consider:

- Could we reach the last index in different ways?
    - Yes. The exact steps we take to reach the last index doesn’t matter because the return type is a simple boolean. The only thing that matters is if we can reach `numbers.length - 1`.
    - However, if the return type was not a boolean, this would be an important consideration!
- Does the number in the last index matter?
    - If we can reach the last index, we can return `true`. As a result, the number of the last index does not matter: it could be 0, or 1, or 2, etc.

## **Coming Up With The Solution**

First, **develop examples**.

Example 1

- `number = []`
- Why this example?
    - It covers an important edge-case we must consider.

Example 2

- `number = [1]`
- Why this example?
    - Similar to an empty array, it covers an important edge-case we must consider.

Example 3

- `number = [0,1,2,3]`
- Why this example?
    - The question states we must start at index 0. The number at index 0 is `0`, which means we can’t progress any further.
    - In other words, **we must be careful of zeroes**! The more you think about zeroes, the more you realize that having a **`0` in the input is the ONLY way you might not reach the last index**. If there exist no zeroes, we will always return `true`!

In an interview, we don’t have a lot of time, so **let’s develop an example that seems to cover various edge-cases**:

- `numbers = [3,1,2,0,1,4]`

This example is nice because:

- We can’t reach the end immediately from the first number.
- There is a 0 in the input, which means there is a possibility of not reaching the end.

---

After spending some time thinking about the problem, **we can make an observation that the time complexity must be at least `O(N)`** because there are examples where we need to visit every element to reach the end. For example: `[1,1,1,1,1]`.

The final solution might be `O(N)`, or it might be something else - we don’t know yet. Nevertheless, we know one thing for sure: we must begin at index 0.

**Let’s begin going through the example `[3,1,2,0,1,4]` by starting at index 0**:

- At index 0, the number is `3`, which means we can jump to index 1, or index 2, or index 3.
    - If we jump to `numbers[3]`, the number is 0, and that means we can’t jump any further.
    - If we jump to `numbers[2]`, the number is 2, and that allows us to jump further.
    - If we jump to `numbers[1]`, the number is 1, and that also allows us to jump further.
- If we jump to index 2 (`numbers[2]`), the number is 2, which means that we can jump to index 3, or index 4.
    - If we jump to `numbers[3]`, the number is 0, and we can’t jump any further.
    - If we jump to `numbers[4]`, we have reached the last index, and we can return `true`!

These steps point us to a common brute-force backtracking solution that takes `O(2^N)` time, `O(N)` space:

- For each index, try every possible jump from the index.
- Recurse the previous step until the last index is reached or a zero value is encountered.
- If the end is reached, return true.
- If zero is encountered, backtrack, and repeat.

```
def jump_game(numbers):
    def jump_game_helper(numbers, index):
        if index == len(numbers) - 1:
            return True
        if index >= len(numbers):
            return False

        for jump in range(1, numbers[index] + 1):
            if jump_game_helper(numbers, index + jump):
                return True

        return False
    
    if len(numbers) == 0:
        return True
    return jump_game_helper(numbers, 0)
```

After a lot of practice, you should be able to find a brute-force solution for most algorithms quickly. As a result, this type of analysis will take only a couple of minutes, and **the main focus of the interview will be finding an efficient solution**.

---

Can we find a more efficient answer?

The backtracking solution is slow because it re-calculates results for indices that we already visited (this is called "overlapping subproblems"). Dynamic programming and memoization are techniques for saving previously computed results and re-using them to eliminate re-calculation.

The following solution is a *slight* modification to the original, and it uses memoization. The time complexity is `O(N^2)`, and the space complexity is `O(N)`.

**We added a `visited` array**, which stores whether we’ve visited the index before. If we have, we return false immediately because we already determined that it’s impossible to reach the end from that index.

```
def jump_game(numbers):
    visited = [False] * len(numbers)
    return jump_game_helper(numbers, 0, visited)

def jump_game_helper(numbers, index, visited):
    if visited[index]:
        return False
    
    if index == len(numbers) - 1:
        return True
    if index >= len(numbers):
        return False

    for jump in range(1, numbers[index] + 1):
        if jump_game_helper(numbers, index + jump, visited):
            return True

    visited[index] = True

    return False
```

---

The quickest solution we have so far is `O(N^2)`. Can we do better?

**Let’s consider other possibilities**:

- `O(N*log(N))`?
    - Usually, `O(N*log(N))` implies sorting or using a data structure such as a heap. Since we can’t change the order of the numbers, and we aren’t trying to find a min or a max, **I wouldn’t give it a high chance that there is a solution that matches this time complexity.**
- `O(N)`?
    - **It is common for dynamic programming questions to have a “greedy" `O(N)` solution.**
- `O(log(N))`?
    - No. We already determined that input like `[1,1,1,1,1]` will always bound the solution to `O(N)`.

`O(N)` seems like the safest bet, so **how can we start thinking about an algorithm that matches such a time complexity?**

Start by making observations:

- `O(N)` means we must be able to loop through the array a constant number of times.
- We must start at index 0.

**If we start at index 0 and then loop through the array, what information can we store at each step that will help us determine if we have a solution at the end?**

Let’s use the example `numbers = [3,1,2,0,1,4]`, and list all the data we have:

- `numbers = [3,1,2,0,1,4]`
- `index = 0`
- `numbers[index] == numbers[0] == 3`
- `lastIndex = numbers.length-1`

Then, **let's break down the information we have**:

- We need to determine if we can reach `lastIndex`
- When we start at index 0, we know the `maxIndex` we can jump to.
    - We can formalize this as `maxIndex = index + numbers[index]`
- The `maxIndex` doesn’t just give us an index we can jump to, it tells us that we can jump to any index *before* `maxIndex`.
    - In other words, as we loop through each index of the array, if we ever encounter an `index` that is *greater than* `maxIndex`, then it means we can’t jump to it, and it means we can’t reach the end.
    - Similarly, if we *can* reach the `lastIndex` without breaking the previous rule, we can return `true`!

> Note: By starting at index 0, we were able to reduce the complexity of the problem. Instead of thinking about the whole array of numbers, we can think about a single number.
> 

This gives us the following pseudocode:

```
index = 0
max_index = 0
while index < len(numbers):
    if index > max_index:
        return False
    max_index = max(max_index, index + numbers[index])
    index += 1

return True
```

> Note: One of the critical lessons from this analysis is how seemingly minor words like max can "hide" a lot of information. You need a certain degree of curiosity to understand the implications of such a word. Treat every keyword in the problem statement as a hint. After a lot of practice, you will notice how one keyword can change the nature of a question.
> 

## **Writing The Solution**

Remember, the hard work should be done by this phase, and now it’s all about making sure no small errors are made.

Final code for the solution:

```
max_index = 0
for i in range(len(numbers)):
    if i > max_index:
        return False
    current_max_index = i + numbers[i]
    max_index = max(max_index, current_max_index)

return True
```

# **Approaching System Design Interviews**

System design interviews require you to lead the conversation and tackle complex problems. The following approach can guide you in excelling at these interviews:

**Mindset and Preparation:**

- Treat the system design interview as a task given by a manager. Lead the conversation confidently.
- Practice within the time constraint of 45 minutes to simulate the real interview scenario.
- Don't rely solely on job experience; practice systematically to ensure a solid performance.

**Problem-Solving Routine:**

- Apply Polya's four-step problem-solving method:
    1. Understand the problem.
    2. Devise a plan (solution).
    3. Carry out the plan (implementation).
    4. Reflect and iterate.

**Understand the Problem:**

- Clarify the problem's scope and details.
- Ask critical questions to gain clarity on the project.
- For a prompt like "design the Twitter mobile app," delve into specifics:
    - Existing infrastructure or new app?
    - API availability and type (REST, GraphQL).
    - Platform (iOS, Android, or both).
    - Features like posting, following, searching, etc.

**Devise a Plan:**

- Translate the problem's requirements into concrete system components.
- Sketch the application flow, even for back-end systems.
- Identify primary user interfaces, buttons, and navigation paths.
- Create a clear picture of how different parts of the system will interact.

**Carry Out the Plan:**

- This step involves detailing the technical aspects of your design.
- Discuss how different components will be implemented and interact.
- Consider databases, APIs, user interfaces, data flow, and more.
- Present your architecture using diagrams, charts, or explanations.

**Look Back and Reflect:**

- Reflect on your design, identifying strengths and weaknesses.
- Consider scalability, fault tolerance, latency, data consistency, etc.
- Analyze trade-offs and areas where you can improve.
- Iterate on your design to address shortcomings.

By following this approach and practicing consistently, you'll build the confidence and skills necessary to excel in system design interviews. Remember, it's about having a structured problem-solving process and demonstrating your ability to design complex systems with efficiency and foresight.
